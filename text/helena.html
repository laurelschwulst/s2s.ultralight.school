About sixty miles north of the buzzing traffic in Brooklyn, a verdant nook lined with a mossy carpet can be found along the trail of the Manitoga Estate in Garrison, New York. Here, the only kind of buzzing comes from the bumble bees feasting upon the woodland’s wildflowers.

In that brief moment of passing, the midday sun hung directly above and I could feel its heat intermittently press upon my head. I held up my phone and snapped three photos in haste before rejoining the estate tour. The glare of the sun threw shapes of varying shades onto the ground. Those shapes swayed with the breeze, patterning the moss. Trees stretched into the sky leaving an overhead clearing, yet the surrounding shrubbery provided ample partitioning from the rest of the world. The main trunk stood with age and weather against the dithering texture of the moss. Around the trunk, tightly clustered buds poked curiously above ground. The undulating surface of this mossy patch furnished onlookers like myself with a yearning to lie down and be cushioned by its natural contours.

When American industrial designer Russel Wright conceived of his residence here in the 1940s, he considered the seventy-five-acre woodland to be an extension of the living room, which he and his guests would’ve accessed by foot along a woodchip trail. We, too, followed those footsteps as we climbed stairs made of boulders and made our way over an engineered waterfall.

As I revisit the photograph a day later on my laptop, the liveliness of the vegetation seems to leap out of the screen. Within each vein and each cell, not only are nutrients being circulated, the desires of the organisms are also being communicated via chemicals. Without our usual instruments to probe and measure, the signals of a cooperative plant society tend to remain hidden from our vision. I thought of Suzanne Simard, the Canadian forestry scientist whose 1997 research on the Wood Wide Web revolutionized our understanding of forest ecology. Trees, as we’ve come to learn, “converse” through fungi networks that lie underground. In an unintended game of telephone, could messages mediated through mycorrhizal filaments ever depart from their original intent? Could there even be fungal dialects that mutate the vernacular? I ponder with admiration what chemical dialogues might’ve taken place to construct such an idyllic pocket of space. I would’ve liked to linger there a little longer.

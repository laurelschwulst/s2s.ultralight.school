The meal has ended. Or maybe just the eating has.

Steak bones curve across white porcelain, occupying more space now than when they were hidden beneath meat. Thick streaks of dark ragù cling stubbornly to broken ribbons of pasta, their edges beginning to dry first. Half-finished glasses of wine catch the warm light from the table lamps and return it to the faces gathered around them. The plates sit exactly as they were left, no longer serving dinner so much as preserving its evidence.

Only one person is still eating.

He stands with one hand on a chair and an apple in the other, taking small bites that seem less governed by hunger than by the rhythm of the conversation. Crunch. He doesn’t chew while anyone else is speaking. More often than not, the apple simply waits. It rises again. Crunch. The apple remains in one hand longer than expected. Attention eventually leaves it, the apple doesn’t.

By then, the boy in blue begins asking to be noticed and has abandoned sitting altogether. He leans every ounce of himself into the back of his mother’s chair until they almost become one shape. Whatever he’s asking for seems urgent only to him. His mother searches through her purse first with only her fingers, continuing to nod along to someone else’s voice. Fingers brush past a tube of lipstick worn silver at the edges. A jingle, keys. A crinkle, folded receipts. The cool edge of a compact mirror. Eventually her eyes surrender and join the search. They don’t seem to help much.

From up here you can’t hear the words, only their confidence. The conversations reveal themselves mostly through gesture. Someone punctuates every sentence with both hands. Another has already begun nodding before the story has reached its ending. At the opposite end of the table, the joke is still making its way there.

The table has changed jobs. Seven elbows rest comfortably on it. Where I’m from, someone would’ve said it before the bread arrived.

Cinco dos.

Sin codos.

Elbows off the table.

Five and two. Seven remain.

Phones, despite possessing the remarkable ability to remove someone from the room entirely, receive no equally catchy arithmetic. These phones seem unusually well-behaved anyway. They cross the table the same way the bread and wine did: briefly, and with somewhere else to go.

No one remembers where the apples came from.

One moment there is only the man standing quietly with one in his hand. Later, everyone is holding one, turning it over, weighing it, as though the weakest point might reveal itself before they even begin. Everyone invents their own technique. Most can’t split one. When someone finally does, the applause is entirely disproportionate to the accomplishment. It reminds me of San Sebastián, where strangers applauded the sunset as though the horizon itself had earned it.

The apples disappear. The laughter doesn’t.

Someone proposes another challenge by scraping a chair back from the table. Lift a chair by one leg using only one hand. Adults accept immediately. The plates stay. The stories drift toward one another, separate, collide, separate again. The table keeps finding new work to do. It has stopped holding dinner.

Vyanka Sotelo is a community builder and creative producer whose work is interested in how movement and shared creative experiences bring people together. She creates spaces for people to move, make, and meet as the Director of Operations at Good Move and Creative Host at RecCreate Collective. Based in Brooklyn, she has created and presented choreography in Domino Park.

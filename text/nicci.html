This is a meditation on touching my personal computing devices, the gestures they ask of me, and the ways they touch me.

Click

My first click was with a mouse.

I’m not picky about my mouse, but I am put off by the “ergonomic” ones — clunky and tough, jagged and angular — as if the more aggressive it is, the more serious you are about your wrist health. My current one is a VssoPlor Type C Wireless Mouse: matte black with a soft sheen and a flatter profile. A silver trim runs down the center, circling the scroll wheel and wrapping around the edge of the mouse, highlighting its teardrop shape. It’s like a little sports car.

My right hand cups the smooth plastic mouse, leaving a delicate pocket of air under my knuckles so as not to smother it. Like a pianist, my left hand rises synchronously, falling on the a-s-d-f keys. The little horizontal bar under my index finger guides the others into position. Everything falls into place like a routine performed a million times since my hands were big enough.

Though my mouse is seamless to the touch, my index and ring fingers find the buttons anyway. The base of my palm rests on whatever I’m using as a mousepad at the moment — today, it’s a thick stack of scrap paper that will get sticky with sweat in a few minutes. The makeshift mousepad raises my right elbow and shoulder so I’m stuck in a lopsided shrug. I feel my shoulders teetering like a seesaw on my spine.

I once worked in an office where Doug Engelbart and the Mother of All Demos quietly haunted every project, so I tried to imagine how the very first mouse felt. Engelbart’s original prototype was a childish wooden block with one tiny, cylindrical red button at the top. The toylike body of the mouse contrasts sharply with the razor-edged wheel it sits on. It’s hard to find any hint of a hand in this proto-mouse.

In Michelangelo’s The Creation of Adam, God reaches his index finger towards Adam, transmitting life, power, and agency to man. The mouse Engelbart uses in the Mother of All Demos is plastic, curved at the base, and has three buttons at the top for his index, middle, and ring fingers. Now with three times the power, Engelbart demonstrates how to put together a grocery list.

What if the mouse, not the screen, were the portal into my digital world? A portal for expressing my agency, or for “perpetrat[ing] this unfulfilled desire, always delayed, always deferred,” as Ali Na says. A click as the smallest unit of agency: keeping the rest of my fingers still, I press my index finger down until it meets solid resistance. My fingertip flexes and reverses its arch. It bends again. And again. And again.

But the mouse is never a part of my computer’s body, only ever a peripheral attachment, so most of the time my clicks happen on a trackpad. The tip of my finger glides across the smooth flat surface, stopping when my eyes stop. I click.

The trackpad is deceitful. It replicates the mouse click, a satisfying dent that springs up when I lift my finger. But there are no springs — for about a decade now, the trackpads on Macbooks have only conjured the illusion of a click, created through sound and haptics. The flatness doesn’t budge.

There’s also something deeply unnatural about the contortions asked of my body as I meet the touchpad with my fingers. The trackpad leans neither left nor right but center — neutral, universal — so I have to origami my body to reach it: fold shoulder, tuck elbow, twist wrist. Each joint nudged and bent into place just to click.

I associate track pads with this dull ache that lingers throughout the day. I can track how much time I’ve spent on my laptop by how sore my neck and right shoulder are by nightfall, while I sit in a crumpled Cheeto shape, watching TV. Cause or effect, it’s hard to tell.

Unlike my grease-stained keyboard, where oily spots have begun to form and letters gradually rub away, my trackpad has no trace of my body nor my touch. But I still feel each click — in my shoulders, in my neck.

Hold

To hold is such a human verb. A hug. The first thing that happens to us when we’re born is to be held.

Controversial psychologist Harry Harlow devised experiments that explored attachment through touch on infant rhesus monkeys. He invented the term “contact comfort” to describe the nourishment and comfort offered by touch that milk alone could not provide.

Every day I hold my phone in my hands, in my pocket, in my bag. It’s not so much a caress as a tight grip, a careless plop, an absent-minded storing away. But it is comforting nonetheless. Research confirms this, finding that having a phone nearby is a reminder that our social world is within reach, and can be a line of defense against awkward situations. Our phones have such a hold on us that our brains even experience “phantom vibration syndrome.”

On my phone screen, to hold is to press. Softness hardens through my action: I tap an icon with my fingertip, then exert pressure and force on an unyielding screen. The solid screen doesn’t cave under my touch. In turn, digital effects compensate. The app icons begin to wiggle, struggling under my fingertip like bugs to be squashed. A haptic thu-thunk.

A digital object has to be held first before it reveals what I can do to it. Hold to cut, hold to delete, hold to rearrange. I pin the pixels down with my finger and they surrender.

Holding connects my life inside a computer to my world outside. I’d see the darkened arch of a tunnel approach and suck in my ribs to hold my breath, nearly passing out as I try to defeat my siblings and get the chance to make a wish. Or we’d play Red Light, Green Light, which in Chinese is “123 木頭人” or “123 wooden person.” I like the Chinese name better — it captures my body abruptly halting itself mid-sprint, solidifying like a wooden mannequin in drawing class that is posed joint by joint. Wobble and I’d get caught.

Holding my pose freezes my body into stillness. Holding my tongue forces me into silence. Harlow’s later, crueler experiments held monkeys captive in isolation chambers, depriving them of touch entirely.

We ask people to wait by holding a thought, with a “please hold!” When I hold down an app icon or a file, there’s a momentary pause, a window of hesitation during which I decide where this gets dragged then dropped. In music, this prolonging is annotated with a fermata — a curved brow over a single dot, like an eye holding me in a staring contest.

A handshake turns into holding hands when we both relax our arms and neither of us lets go. A hug becomes a hold when we ease into each other and stay there. A click amplifies into a hold when my finger presses against the screen for 500 milliseconds. My phone holds me back.

Nicci Yin is an interaction designer and artist whose work asks what else computing could feel like. She makes conceptual art about software in the form of prototypes, videos, writing, and workshops. Her work has appeared in the Are.na Annual and The HTML Review. She is based between Seattle and Taipei.

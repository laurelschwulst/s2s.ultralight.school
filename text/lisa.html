“When’s the corn ready,” I text my mom. “When are you doing corn?”

“The corn is ready when the corn is ready,” she says. “I don’t know. Hopefully two weeks, maybe a week and a half. It looks a little young to me, what to do you think,” she asks. She sends a photo. Underdeveloped kernels the size of my baby's tiny teeth lay exposed, the husk turned back.

The corn is not ready. “Ready for what?” one may ask. Ready to do. Ready to be done. I don't know what people call it in other places, but here we do corn. There is field corn for the cows, and there is sweet corn for the people. We do sweet corn, we combine field corn, but that doesn't come until much later in the year.

We watch the weather, walk out the field lane, hold the ears, trace the spaces between the kernels with a thumb, and when it is time, some weekend in late July or early August, connections both close and tenuous will descend on the farm.

“When's the corn ready,” they've already asked.

“When are you doing corn.”

“Can I get some corn, can I come pick it.”

“Just a couple dozen.”

“Just six dozen.”

“Just a few dozen to do with my mom sister aunt daughter.”

The sweet corn is ready when it feels full in your hand, when you grab a fistful of husk, yank it back, brush away the clinging silk, run your fingers over the exposed kernels. They are thick with a slight sticky dampness. Their surfaces are tender, fit to burst; they’ll squirt corn juice if you jam a thumbnail into them. The ear should not feel bumpy or irregular. If it does, this ear is poorly pollinated and not a good proxy for the whole field. Choose another, then another. Be sure to grab the sticky silk, dry and brown at the ends, while pulling hard on the husk to reveal the color and girth of the kernels, the ear itself. Brush lightly over the kernels. Stare at the ear, yellow as the sun itself, as the butter that would drip into the crevices if you were eating it off the cob. Hold it up into the dappled light of the cornfield, that green-saturated light from the stalks and leaves that stealthily cut your arms, and admire its contours and its weight.

The only breakfast fit for doing corn is a glazed and craggy sour cream donut, a chipped mug (familiar since childhood) of Maxwell House on the edge of a picnic table, on top of an overturned barn bucket, on the back of the lawn mower that happens to be sitting nearby. Any halfway flat surface will do, because you are doing corn and all other bodily wants, preferences, and needs are now on pause until the corn is done.

We all know our roles, though they shift as we age. We all know who’s picking, husking, cooking, cutting, bagging.

I cut the corn, knives the sharper the better, taking off just the top of the fat kernels. This becomes mesmerizing, holding the ear in the non-dominant hand, sliding the knife under the tops of the kernels, rotating the ear, then scraping the insides of the kernels into the bowl to be reunited with the rest. Friends in the city to whom you feed this corn will say, “ah, I see it's creamed,” but you know the truth. This is not creamed corn. It’s corn you did, on the farm, with your family, the way you’ve done corn your whole life, and the way your family has done corn their whole lives. This is not a whole kernel situation, this corn cannot be charred for salsa. It can be deposited into plastic freezer bags, laid flat and smooth, frozen to eat in soup, as a side, blended (there’s your creamed!) for winter holidays’ baked corn. This is utility corn, saved to last for a casserole you’d eat on a farm until it’s time to do corn next year.

Next year! Every year, my mom declares she’s not doing this much next year. How much? I do not know. Before me, boxes of gleaming kerneled cobs wait to be thrown into the granite pot over the propane burner. Coolers of ice water line the porch to cool the cobs after they’re cooked. I need more corn! My brother takes a break from cooking to fish the iciest ears out of the coolers filled with ice water, stand them upright in a metal bowl, so the water drips off before I cut.

For hours I sit in this chair gripping an endless stream of ear after ear in my left hand, cutting with my right. Except for when I don’t. Except for when I dump a cob bucket, fetch a snack for the baby, fish ears out of ice water, my hand lingering around the cob to detect any heat radiating from within. Time stretches and snaps, like my wrist changing directions between cut and scrape. Toward and away.

Back in the chair, I reach for another ear. I grip it and pause. This cob’s warm! This one’s still warm, I tell my brother. He reaches deeper into the ice water next time, screeching as his arm turns numb. “We’re almost done,” we say, until someone points out another box the rest of us hadn’t noticed.

Sticky, from forehead to ankle.

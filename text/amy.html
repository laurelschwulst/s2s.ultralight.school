To watch, with eyes half-open, the spider weaving its web.

{1}

While ensnared, enamored, in a display of surrender, suspended mid-air, tethered by a whispering thread. Words
make their way from me to you, you to me.
The sound wedges itself between still panes of glass.

{2}

{3}

In the low light I find my breath to be composed of only contours, outlines, slender markings;
the outside air darkens the wisps of hair at the nape of your neck. I take a strand. Wrap it tightly around my finger until my skin turns white.

{4}

{5}

{6}

We don’t treat our problems with poison — you say — and so they multiply in our sleep.
One by one, mother and child, dancing across pale yellow skies:
scattered, scurrying motions evidenced by the slow scrawl across the ceiling
through the dust now etched into the window screens
to the crevice right below the bathroom sink. The wind blows right through each opening.

{7}

{8}

I watch the silken threads of each web snap apart in the breeze. Swaying, moving as if in agitation,

{9}

or perhaps in ecstasy, or

{10}

swaying to the tap tap tapping of your fingers against my bare skin, the rain now drumming a slightly syncopated beat. The spiders above our heads form charcoal streaks, trailing through the air.

Amy Fang is a Brooklyn-based artist and designer who works across artist books, ambient text, and sculptural drawings. She grew up sketching still lifes and landscapes with her grandfather—who taught her by clasping his hand around hers and guiding her movements across the page—and later came to understand the computer as another kind of prosthetic, a tool that enables forms of guided improvisation. Her practice often engages with thinking through gesture, touch, and the extension of the hand into space.

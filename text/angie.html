The prompt sits open for most of the course: Translate sound in some way, for readers to experience without hearing it.

Opening JJJJJerome Ellis’s pages, looking at how they suspend time,
the space between word and breath keeps thinning.

I put on Journey by Austin Wintory
because I know what it does to my body.
Track number 2, The Call, 0:37.

Whirlpools and vast space, settling on my sternum,
I wait for the release with my shoulders.

放空, my mum tells me,
empty out and leave some space.

I close my eyes and trace the grainy static behind my eyelids —
flickering steel wool, a low hum in the colour of oxidization.
I can taste the metallic speckles on the back of my tongue,
cold and sour, like how I imagine the inside of a battery might reverb.

Clusters gather, pitch rising as they tighten, dense enough to collide.
Every time I zoom in, they disperse, folding back somewhere else.
Clicks land near my cerebellum, sharp against my teeth.
I wonder if this is what people see when they talk about phosphenes.

Angie Pai is a Taiwanese-Australian artist exploring sensory processing and diasporic memory. Informed by affective neuroscience, her practice considers how intergenerational exchange continually reshapes somatic regulation and cultural transmission. She has presented across Melbourne, Sydney, Taichung, and Taipei, with support from Creative Australia, Creative Victoria, the Ian Potter Cultural Trust, Sydney Opera House, and Diversity Arts Australia.

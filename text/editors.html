Dear reader,

This book came out of a class from Ultralight School called “Sense to Sense,” a writing and publishing course held during July and August, 2026.

This year was our second time teaching the class, and while our inaugural course was held in-person in Brooklyn in 2025, changing circumstances (new life, new job, new city) led us to move the class online this summer. Despite the change in location, the premise for the class remained more or less the same: over five weeks, we explored the five senses through reading discussions and writing prompts that asked us to translate sensorial experiences into written language.

Moving the class online made some things easier. For one, participants could join from anywhere, we were no longer limited to being local to New York. But moving solely to a digital space was also an interesting challenge for a class about the senses, which are seemingly so tied to the physical world. As our friend Toph Tucker once said in an interview we did with him, “I’m a body! I’m not just a brain in a vat.” This can be weirdly hard to remember online, where senses like sight and sound are prioritized, but smell, touch, and taste don’t quite transmit. And then there was the challenge of creating a group publication — always a wonderfully messy, cacophonous process — without being in the same room.

We attempted to get around these constraints using various strategies, usually involving snail mail. For a writing prompt for translating smell into atmospheric fragrance reviews, Liza Pittard (a participant and also a perfumer) sent scent samples to people’s homes. Another participant, Leah Koransky, riso-printed class-designed bookmarks/inserts for these books, also sent through the mail. Many a doc, folder, Are.na channel, and spreadsheet were also employed. But mostly we found that working online, at a distance, only enhanced our self-initiated task to translate the felt, smelled, heard, tasted, and seen, the ineffable and intangible, into language rich enough for others to experience what we experienced. Or at least, to generate some interesting writing in the attempt.

Our class participants came from all over the world. We noticed a “5-ness” to the whole class, beautifully pointed out by participant Yo-Yo Lin. Of course, there were the five senses and five corresponding weeks of class, but also five timezones (EST, PST, CST, GMT+8, PHT) among participants and five times of day (morning, noon, afternoon, evening, night) with which we associated our fragrances. There are also five petals on the flower emoji we use when signing off our emails 🌸.

And like the name “Sense to Sense,” we also noticed a duality 🌸🌸 this year: we met at 7 p.m. EST for all sessions. But for some of us, this 7 p.m. was 7 a.m., as this year three friends (Anna Kuo, Angie Pai, and Yo-Yo Lin) joined us from Taiwan, waking up bright and early in Taipei, on the other side of the world, to join our class. Also joining us for her morning, Jianna So beamed in from Manilla.

When orchestrating the publication, we invited everyone to join various teams — “design,” “insert,” and “editorial,” for instance. We also created a couple new teams this year — “audio” and “web” — since it felt only appropriate to take advantage of these opportunities as a class connecting through the world wide web. We kicked off the book making by inviting two members of last year’s design team, Ariana Gupta and Alicia Liu, to share their wonderful work, allowing us to build upon it. The teams worked well together, and after the first round of presentations we noticed an overall theme emerging: There was the design team’s visible layering of annotation, notes, and handwriting over our texts, and also the layering of sound, image, and text in the insert, audio, and web teams’ directions. We loved how this layering brought to the surface the process of making and thinking together, and added tactility to our digital class (“touch from a distance”).

We noticed many of the pieces in our publication were naturally multisensorial, somehow without thinking, blending multiple senses into writing. We wonder if it’s a type of balancing or compensation that happens when conveying senses virtually — opening a portal through the screen.

For our last virtual class on Zoom, we celebrated with a “cheers,” each going around and sharing our special beverage, each in its own special vessel — glass, cup, or conch shell (shout out to Vyanka Sotelo). This closing ritual was inspired by a prompt by Yo-Yo Lin: as a way to tie our work together, each participant was asked to translate their published piece into a written description of a hypothetical vessel. Vessels were “round and irregular…leaky” (Liz Ellis), a “hand-hammered bowl” (Angie Pai), a “puffy plate” with a rim “like the crust of a pizza” (Helena Dong), an “abstract muted speaker” (April Jung), a shallow bowl resting “on four bumpy legs” (Nicci Yin), a ceramic cup with “rough spikes that line its edge” (Anna Kuo), “a deep and wide bowl shape” (Lisa Yoder), “a flat circular bowl with the shortest possible rim” (Sarah Chekfa), “a spindly yet symmetrical vase” (Amy Fang). Later Yo-Yo vessels from these descriptions, a motif that appears throughout our publication...

Practically speaking, a vessel is a container (such as a cup, bowl, glass, or bottle) for holding something, often a liquid. A vessel can also be a large boat or ship, and maybe that’s why we’ve described this editor’s letter as a rather wide and shallow bowl; one that’s naturally open to the sky, able to contain everything and everyone who has taken part in the class. We truly hope Sense to Sense has been a special and useful vessel for everyone involved, perhaps even transportive, taking us to a new way of understanding the world.

🌸🌸

Amidst the flowers,

Laurel & Meg

Meg Miller is a writer and editor living in Richmond, Virginia who has contributed writing to the New York Times, Frieze, BOMB, Los Angeles Review of Books, The Atlantic, and other web and print publications, mostly about the ways design, art, language, and technology shape culture and society. She’s editorial director at Are.na and teaches at Virginia Commonwealth University.

Laurel Schwulst is an artist, writer, and technologist. She teaches design at Washington University in St. Louis and also directs Ultralight School. Previously Laurel taught at Yale and Princeton, co-authored a book of fragrance reviews, and held design roles at Linked by Air, Kickstarter, and Are.na.
